Student's t-Distribution Convergence to Standard Normal N(0, 1)

Demonstrating how heavier Student's t tails merge into the Standard Gaussian curve as sample size n increases

William Sealy Gosset's 1908 Discovery ("Student"): When estimating the population mean μ using the sample standard deviation s instead of true σ, small samples introduce extra stochastic uncertainty. This creates heavy/fat tails in the t-distribution. As sample size n grows (and degrees of freedom ν = n - 1 → ∞), Slutsky's theorem guarantees that s → σ and the t-distribution becomes mathematically indistinguishable from N(0, 1).

Why This Matters in Clinical Research:

When conducting clinical trials with small cohorts (e.g. pilot neurosurgical studies with n < 30), using the Gaussian critical value z = 1.960 instead of Gosset's t-critical cutoff leads to serious Type I error inflation (false positives). For n = 4, testing at z = 1.96 yields an actual false-positive rate of 14.5% rather than the intended 5.0%. Once sample size reaches n ≥ 31 (ν ≥ 30), t_crit drops to 2.042 (only 4.2% wider than 1.960), making the normal approximation clinically acceptable.

Two-Sample Overlap, Dispersion (SD vs. SEM) & Alpha Significance Boundary

Explore how sample curves overlap and separate as SD, SEM (via n), and difference (Δ) change, why α = 0.05 defines significance, and what happens when α is adjusted

The Fundamental Distinction Between SD, SEM, and Alpha (α): Standard Deviation (SD) measures real biological variability between individual patients—it does not shrink as sample size grows. Standard Error (SEM = SD/√n) measures our uncertainty in the mean—it shrinks steadily as n increases. Two patient populations can overlap by 70% (large SD), yet their treatment means can separate completely (small SEM). Significance level α sets our tolerance for false-positive claims (Type I error). At α = 0.05, the required separation boundary is 1.96 standard errors. Tightening α to 0.01 pushes this boundary out to 2.58 SEs; relaxing α to 0.10 pulls it inward to 1.65 SEs.

Sample Design Guide: Paired vs. Independent

Independent Samples (Between-Subjects / Unpaired):

Used when comparing two distinct, mutually exclusive cohorts of subjects (e.g. Treatment Group vs. Placebo Control, Surgical Technique A vs. Technique B, Males vs. Females). Each observation belongs to only one group.

  • Parametric tests: Student's t-test (if equal variances) or Welch's t-test (if unequal variances).
  • Non-parametric test: Mann-Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank-sum).
Paired Samples (Within-Subjects / Dependent / Matched):

Used when the same subjects are measured twice under two conditions or time points (e.g. Pre-treatment vs. Post-treatment ICP, Baseline vs. 6-Month Outcome, Left vs. Right hemisphere of same patient, or matched pairs).

  • Each row represents the same subject (Observation i in Group 1 pairs directly with Observation i in Group 2).
  • Analyzes within-subject differences (Δi = Posti − Prei), removing inter-subject variability.
  • Parametric test: Paired Samples Student's t-test (assumes normal distribution of differences).
  • Non-parametric test: Wilcoxon Signed-Rank test (for skewed or ordinal differences).
📊

Multi-Group Study Design Guide: Between-Subjects vs. Repeated Measures

Independent Samples (Between-Subjects / Unpaired):

Used when comparing three or more independent cohorts of subjects (e.g. Surgical Technique A vs. Technique B vs. Conservative Management, or 3 distinct disease phenotypes). Each patient belongs to exactly one cohort.

  • Parametric + Equal Variances: Classic One-Way ANOVA (Fisher's F-test) with Tukey's HSD post-hoc pairwise contrasts.
  • Parametric + Unequal Variances: Welch's Robust ANOVA (df adjusted via Welch-Satterthwaite) with Games-Howell post-hoc contrasts.
  • Non-Parametric (Skewed / Ordinal): Kruskal-Wallis H Test with Dunn's pairwise contrasts (Bonferroni-adjusted).
Repeated Measures (Within-Subjects / Matched Longitudinal):

Used when the same cohort of subjects is evaluated across three or more sequential conditions or longitudinal time points (e.g. Baseline T0, 1-Month T1, 6-Month T2, 1-Year T3).

  • Each cohort column must have the same sample size (Row i represents Subject i tracked over time).
  • Partitions inter-subject variance (SSSubjects) away from residual error (SSError), substantially boosting statistical power.
  • Parametric test: One-Way Repeated Measures ANOVA with Sphericity assessment and Greenhouse-Geisser (ε̂) degrees of freedom correction.
  • Non-Parametric test: Friedman Rank Sum Test with pairwise Wilcoxon Signed-Rank post-hoc contrasts and Kendall's W concordance coefficient.
